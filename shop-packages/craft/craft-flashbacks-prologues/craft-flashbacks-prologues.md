# Flashbacks and prologues

## Light

Job: deliver complex past only when dramatization earns the interrupt — otherwise weave context in the present.

Contract: flashbacks and prologues create hunger or turn the main plot; if removable with no loss, cut them.

Must-not-break:
- Choose form first: context / memory / full flashback
- Flashback answers curiosity already hooked — not expo dump
- Smooth in/out without lantern phrases (“Back in the present…”)
- Prologue creates questions, not answers; never a patch for weak Ch.1
- Complex past suits simpler main plots (or you drown)

## Normal

**Complex vs simple backstory**
- **Simple:** past incidental to plot/arc → weave lightly; no special device needed
- **Complex:** past drives main plot (complicated motivations, secrets between characters, secrets from self, author withholding for reveal)
- Share as **context** (need-to-know world facts) or **reveal** (pivot that changes perception)
- Rule of thumb: complex backstories suit simpler main plots; complex plots often need simpler backstories

Five technique families (Weiland series): prologue, flashback, backstory-as-story, alternating timelines, linear weave. First two below; rest = alternatives.

**Three backstory forms (choose before dramatizing)**
1. **Context** — woven into forward story; often invisible (prefer ~80–85% of needs)
2. **Memory** — character recalls while still in present-scene action
3. **Flashback** — past scene presented “live”; fully interrupts main story (cayenne — little spice, excess overwhelms)

Ask: what makes full dramatization worth the interrupt risks?

Flashback earns its keep when:
- Essential defining past (wound / formative event) material to *this* journey
- Secret/reveal central enough to warrant full scene
- Brief insert that heightens present stakes/meaning (often a few paragraphs)

**When flashbacks work**
Advantages: visceral, detailed; complicated events fully experienced.
Disadvantages: stop main plot; demand patience; may dump new cast.

Tips:
- Short usually best — especially late with no foreshadowed time-shift
- Long flashback must carry its own drama; longer → more dramatic required
- Not exposition dumps — answers to curiosity already hooked; new info should turn the main plot
- Must move story forward *inside* the flashback *and* by its placement in the main timeline
- Placement: “what does the reader need to know and when?” — overload before investment kills impact
- Opening-story flashback is hardest (enter room looking backward; false promise of story subject) — if used, ruthlessly short and deliberate

Failure pattern: long mid-story episode centered on barely introduced antagonist → audience leaves the relationship they care about for strangers.

**Smooth in / out (no lanterns)**
Avoid: “She remembered it as if it were yesterday…” / “scene unfolded like a movie…” / “Back in the present…” / “She shook her head to clear the memory.”

Prefer:
- Anchor flashback to something in the current moment
- Standalone past scene: space break; still segue so it ties to main story without announcing the device
- Memory is slideshow, not full movie — convention works only if it doesn’t feel like a device yanking the dream

**When prologues work**
Forms: flash-forward; atmospheric hook + foreshadow; most often mini-flashback of crucial past before Chapter 1.

Advantages: set stage without foreshadowing; can frame the reveal the book builds toward; “short, quick, dirty” so main plot runs clean.
Disadvantages: live apart from main narrative → bump past→present; can destroy subtext you’d earn by withholding; force reader to **begin the story twice**.

Must create questions, not dump answers. Momentum = need to know how consequences play out. If removable and story still sings → start at Chapter 1.

**Prologue jobs that earn the page**
- Foreshadow / set mystery (hint ending → hunger for how A→B)
- Catalyst event remote in time/place from Ch.1 (else it may just *be* Ch.1)
- Need-to-know background with clear impact on central conflict (riskiest — boredom trap)
- Character motivation via formative vignette (childhood wound that makes later choices visceral)
- Dramatic irony: reader sees hanging/crime/secret characters don’t yet know
- Unique/minor POV that main cast can’t convey (e.g. GRRM-style cold open)
- Tone/theme (ideally not *only* tone — Ch.1 can do that)
- Voice intro for distinctive first-person narrator

**Prologue don’ts**
- Not a substitute for weak Ch.1 hook — appetizer can’t fix bad first course; bait-and-switch burns goodwill
- Not research dump, setting-only tour, or backstory recap with no inciting pressure
- Not Chapter 1 wearing a hat / duplicate of what Ch.1 will do
- Not for author pleasure — for reader intrigue
- Keep brief (often ~1–2 pages in contemporary practice); long antechamber before promised story

Tests:
1. What work does it do? What’s sacrificed vs jumping in?
2. Could this be Ch.1 or woven later?
3. Does it do what a chapter can’t?
4. Does it color/enhance what follows?

| Failure | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Flashback = lore dump | Hook curiosity first; cut to turning info |
| Lantern transitions | Anchor to present object/sensory cue |
| Prologue answers everything | Leave consequence hunger |
| Long cold antagonist episode | Stay with cared-for relationship; shorten |
| Removable prologue | Start at Ch.1 |

## Hard

**Alternatives when flashback/prologue is wrong tool**
- Linear weave: context + memory in present scenes 
- Alternating timelines: foreshadowed dual tracks instead of sudden long flashback
- Dual-timeline novel braid (equal tracks): 
- Backstory-as-story: the past *is* the novel (or equal half)
- Dialogue / action implication with genuine information gap
- Cut: if present story works without it, prefer never
- Frame / nested tell: when the *telling* itself has stakes 

**Prologue vs Chapter 1 diagnostic**
| Question | If yes… |
|----------|---------|
| Is the catalyst remote in time/place from the main cast’s start? | Prologue candidate |
| Same place/cast/pressure as the opening? | Make it Ch.1 |
| Does it only set tone/world? | Fold into Ch.1 hook |
| Does removing it kill a planted mystery or irony? | Keep; ensure Ch.1 still hooks |
| Does it spoil the reveal the book earns? | Cut or shorten to question-only |

**Flashback placement advanced**
- Mid-book long form needs its own Want + turn; treat as a nested scene 
- Late flashback without foreshadowed time-shift → keep ruthlessly short
- Opening flashback: highest false-promise risk — default against unless the “present” frame is clearly secondary
- Multiple flashbacks: escalate relevance; repeating the same wound beat dulls impact 

**When to break the “avoid prologue” folklore**
- Genre expectation (epic fantasy cold open; thriller crime-before-credits) can license a short prologue *if* Ch.1 still hooks
- Dramatic irony that Ch.1 POV cannot hold may require a disposable/minor POV prologue
- Still apply the remove-and-test: goodwill costs of a second beginning remain real (WD “prohibition” debate)
