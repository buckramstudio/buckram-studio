# Dialect and accent on the page

Suggestion vs phonetic spelling — readability, character, and respect.

## Light

Job: place region/class/L2 speech without drowning the reader or mocking the speaker.

Contract: signal dialect; don’t transcribe every phoneme.

Must-not-break:
- Diction + rhythm + idiom beat apostrophe storms
- One or two recurring markers > full phonetic cast
- If reader must decode every line, pace dies
- Avoid eye-dialect that only “others” marginalized speakers
- Pick a marking level and keep it consistent
- Delivery of other languages: 

## Normal

**Prefer signal over transcription**
- Grammar / word order / loanwords can place class, region, L2 English without mocking spelling
- Narrator report once (“soft Galway lilt”) → then trust dialogue texture
- Speech quirks catalog: (~2 quirks per character)

**Eye dialect (named craft term)**
- Coined by George Philip Krapp (1925): spellings that violate the *eye*, not the ear (`frunt` for *front*) — nudge of “nonstandard,” often comic/superiority
- Broader use: any nonstandard spelling for speech, including true pronunciation spellings (`dat` for *that*)
- Most authors should sprinkle, not phonetically cast entire dialogue
- May appear in letters/diaries to mark education level — still a stereotype risk

**Readability and fairness**
- Jane Raymond Walpole: syntax, punctuation, colloquial/regional word choice often cue dialect better than orthography
- Dufresne / Columbia Guide: avoid eye dialect — frequently pejorative (stupid vs regional) and distracting; prefer rhythm, syntax, diction, idiom, local vocabulary
- Denis R. Preston (sociolinguistics): respellings can denigrate speaker as boorish/uneducated
- Frank Nuessel: eye dialect interacts with and reinforces group stereotypes

**Code-switch**
- Gloss only what POV would gloss; context carries the rest
- Full untranslated lines need emotional or plot payoff

| Failure | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Comic phonetic pile-up | Cut to idiom + one marker |
| Accent only for “othered” cast | Mark power speakers too, or mark none |
| Unreadable stretch dialogue | Standard spelling + rhythm |
| Dialect as sole personality | Add Want, status, subtext |

## Hard

**Diagnostic spectrum**
| Technique | Effect | Risk |
|-----------|--------|------|
| Standard spelling + idiom/syntax | Readable place/class | Weak signal if too subtle |
| Light grammatical nonstandard (`ain’t`, `them` for *those*) | Fast “popular speech” cue (Krapp) | Class caricature if piled on |
| True pronunciation spelling | Accents ear hears | Indecipherable; outsider exoticizing |
| Eye dialect proper (`enuff`, `wos`) | Comic/literary nudge | Pejorative superiority bond author↔reader vs speaker |
| Font / typography tricks (comics, Pratchett) | Character brand | Prose novels rarely have this tool |

**Edge cases**
- Dickens-style dense dialect: historical precedent ≠ modern default — expect fatigue and stereotype readings
- Consistent heavy respelling can make speech indecipherable to readers unfamiliar with the accent
- Satiric misspelling aimed at *powerful* speakers (Betjeman police) differs ethically from mocking marginalized speech — still craft-loud
- Free indirect: narrative voice should not suddenly “correct” a character’s mind unless POV demands it 
- Kidlit / sensitivity: dialect care pairs with , 

**When phonetic marking earns keep**
- Rare comic/caricature contracts that own the bit
- Brief cue then drop to suggestion
- Character’s own semi-literate writing (document voice) — still check stereotype load
